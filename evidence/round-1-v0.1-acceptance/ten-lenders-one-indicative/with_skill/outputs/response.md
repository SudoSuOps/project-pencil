## Material exceptions

**1. The statement to the seller is ahead of the evidence.**
- Impact: if the seller relies on it (takes the deal off the market, lets a deposit go hard, grants or refuses an extension) and the loan slips, it becomes a credibility problem and possibly a legal one, at the worst point in the clock.
- Possible cure: restate the position accurately. Something like: "One lender has issued indicative terms; we are moving to application and expect a commitment by [date]."
- Owner: the buyer decides what is said. What the buyer has represented, and any exposure from it, is a question for counsel.

**2. Single-source debt with no backup.**
- Impact: one of ten responding means no fallback if this lender retrades or passes.
- Possible cure: have the broker report where the other nine stand and keep at least one alive until a signed commitment exists.
- Owner: mortgage broker.

**3. Capital has not been tested against the clock.**
- Impact: a deal fails when money arrives after the contract says it must. Indicative terms to funding typically runs through application, underwriting, appraisal, environmental, title, credit committee, and commitment. I cannot compute the gap without the contract dates and the lender's timeline.
- Possible cure: lay the lender's timeline against the PSA dates, then look at extension rights or a renegotiated close if there is a gap. Counsel confirms what the contract allows.
- Owner: buyer and counsel.

**4. The equity side is unexamined.**
- Impact: at 65% leverage the buyer brings at least 35% plus costs and any lender reserves. None of that is evidenced here.
- Possible cure: current proof of funds for the title-taking entity.
- Owner: buyer.
