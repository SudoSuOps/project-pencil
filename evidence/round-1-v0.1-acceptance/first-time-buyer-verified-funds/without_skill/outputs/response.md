**Assessment: financial capacity is established, and authority is nearly established. The missing track record is not a capacity problem.**

This rests on what you've described; I haven't seen the letter or the resolution.

## Financial capacity: established, subject to three checks

A bank letter that counsel authenticated directly with the bank is the strongest proof of funds short of money in escrow. $9M against a $7.2M all-cash price is 125% coverage. That leaves $1.8M for closing costs, prorations and reserves, with no financing contingency.

Three things on the face of the letter decide whether that holds:

1. **Account holder.** An LLC formed last month often has no funded account yet. If the money sits with the managing member or a parent, the LLC does not have $9M. You would then need the funds moved into the LLC, or a commitment or guaranty from the account holder.
2. **What "available" means.** Unrestricted cash on deposit is clean. An undrawn credit line, a securities account or pledged funds is weaker and should be described as such.
3. **Date.** The letter shows one day's balance. It should be recent, and it can be refreshed before the deposit goes hard.

## Authority: nearly established

A signed resolution naming the managing member as signatory is the right document. It is only as good as the authority of whoever adopted it, so confirm:

- **Operating agreement.** The resolution was adopted by the people the agreement requires, and no member consent threshold applies to a purchase this size.
- **Existence.** Certificate of formation and a current good standing certificate from the formation state.
- **Foreign qualification.** If the property is in a different state, registration there, where that state requires it.

The title company will ask for all of this at closing anyway. Counsel should confirm the exact requirements.

## Track record: an execution risk, not a capacity gap

Capacity means the buyer legally exists, the signer can bind it, and the money is there. Experience is not part of that test. With all cash there is also no lender who could decline a first-time sponsor.

The broker's real worry is whether a new buyer will get through diligence and close without retrading. Deal terms address that better than a résumé:

- **Deposit.** A meaningful sum (2–3% or more) that goes hard early, or partly hard at signing.
- **Timeline.** Short diligence and a quick close, which all cash makes credible.
- **Disclosure.** Who the principals are and where the funds came from. The seller and the title company will want this for a brand-new entity.
- **Team.** The counsel, inspectors and other third parties already engaged, which shows experienced people are running the process.
- **Backstop.** If the funds are not in the LLC's own account, a principal's guaranty of the purchase obligations.

## What to tell the seller's broker

The buyer has bank-verified liquidity of 125% of the price, no financing contingency and a documented signatory. If the letter is in the LLC's name and the operating agreement supports the resolution, I would call capacity verified without qualification. The deposit and timeline terms then deal with the remaining concern.
