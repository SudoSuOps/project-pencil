**Assessment: capacity to close is substantially established, with two points to confirm.** The broker's concern is about track record, which is a different question from capacity, and it is best answered with deal terms rather than more paperwork.

## What the evidence supports

- **Funds.** $9M verified against a $7.2M all-cash price is 125% coverage. After closing costs, there is still roughly $1.6M of cushion (assuming costs of 1-2% of price). Counsel authenticating the letter directly with the bank removes the usual forged-letter risk.
- **No financing risk.** All cash means no lender approval, appraisal or loan timeline to fail, which is where most first-time buyers fall down.
- **Authority.** A signed resolution naming the managing member as signatory is the right document.
- **New LLC.** An entity formed last month is not a red flag in itself. Single-purpose entities formed for an acquisition are standard practice, including for experienced buyers.

## What to confirm before calling it clean

1. **Whose account holds the $9M?** If the letter names the LLC, capacity is established. If the funds sit with the managing member, a parent or an investor, the LLC itself has nothing yet. In that case you need a commitment from the account holder to fund the LLC, or the money moved into the LLC's account.
2. **Does the resolution bind the LLC?** Check it against the operating agreement and the state filing:
   - it is signed by whoever the agreement requires (all members, a majority, or the manager);
   - the managing member's authority covers a purchase of this size;
   - the entity is in good standing.

Two smaller checks:

- **Letter wording.** It should be recent and say the funds are liquid and unencumbered, not just that a balance or relationship exists.
- **Ownership and source of funds.** Expect the title or escrow company to ask who the beneficial owners are and where the money came from. Have that ready.

## Answering the broker

The seller's real exposure is that a new LLC has no other assets. If it defaults, the deposit is all the seller can collect. Terms that address this directly:

- **A larger deposit**, 5-10% of price instead of a token amount, in escrow at signing.
- **A deposit that goes non-refundable early**, either at the end of a short due diligence period or in part from day one.
- **A short timeline**, for example 21-30 days of due diligence and closing 15 days later.
- **A package for the seller**: the bank letter, counsel's written confirmation that it was authenticated, the resolution, and a good-standing certificate.
- **A refreshed proof of funds** just before the deposit goes non-refundable.
- **Optionally, a principal's guaranty** of the buyer's obligations, limited to the pre-closing period, if the seller still hesitates.

Lack of track record is execution risk: whether the buyer will run diligence competently and not try to renegotiate. Experienced counsel and a named diligence team help there. It does not bear on whether the buyer can pay.

## Bottom line

If the letter is in the LLC's name (or backed by a funding commitment) and the resolution checks out against the operating agreement, this buyer is a lower closing risk than a seasoned buyer who needs a loan. A meaningful deposit that goes non-refundable early should settle the broker's concern.
