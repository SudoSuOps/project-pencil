# Response to Turn 1

**Short answer: it pencils on the debt, thinly on the equity, if the T-12 is real.** At $18.5M on $1,240,000 of NOI you are buying a **6.70% cap**. Whether that works depends on property type, market and your debt quote, none of which I have, so the numbers below rest on assumed financing.

**Assumed financing:** 65% LTV, 6.50% rate, 30-year amortization, no closing costs or reserves.

| Metric | Result |
|---|---|
| Going-in cap rate | 6.70% |
| Loan (65% LTV) | $12,025,000 |
| Equity | $6,475,000 |
| Annual debt service (7.58% constant) | $912,074 |
| DSCR | 1.36x |
| Debt yield | 10.3% |
| Cash flow after debt service | $327,926 |
| Cash-on-cash | 5.1% |

**How to read it**

- **Financeable:** a 1.36x DSCR and 10.3% debt yield clear the usual lender minimums (1.25x and roughly 9-10%), so 65% leverage should be available.
- **Leverage barely helps:** the 6.70% cap is only 20 bps above the 6.50% rate and sits below the 7.58% loan constant. Amortizing debt drags the cash yield down to 5.1%, below the unlevered 6.70%.
- **The return has to come from growth:** a 5.1% cash-on-cash only works if you have a credible path to higher NOI (rent bumps, mark-to-market, lease-up) or you are pricing in a lower exit cap.

**What would change the answer**

- **The T-12 itself:** I have taken $1,240,000 at face value. One-time income, under-market management fees, missing reserves or a tax reassessment on sale would each lower it.
- **Your actual debt terms:** every 25 bps of rate moves cash-on-cash by roughly 30-35 bps at this leverage.
- **Market cap rate for the asset type:** 6.70% is rich for some property types and thin for others.

Send me the property type, market, your debt quote and the rent roll or lease terms, and I can firm this up into a proper underwriting.
