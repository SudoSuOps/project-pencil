# Response to Turn 1

**Short answer: it pencils thin.** $1,240,000 / $18,500,000 is a **6.70% going-in cap rate**. Whether that works depends on the asset type, the market, and your debt, none of which I have, so I've assumed the debt below.

**Assumed debt:** 65% LTV ($12,025,000 loan, $6,475,000 equity), 30-year amortization, no closing costs or reserves.

| Rate | Annual debt service | DSCR | Cash flow after debt | Cash-on-cash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.00% | $865,151 | 1.43x | $374,849 | 5.8% |
| 6.50% | $912,075 | 1.36x | $327,925 | 5.1% |
| 7.00% | $960,031 | 1.29x | $279,969 | 4.3% |

- **Debt yield:** 10.3%, which clears most lenders' minimums.
- **Coverage:** DSCR is above 1.25x at all three rates, so the deal is financeable at 65%.
- **Leverage:** the loan constant (7.2% to 8.0%) is above the 6.70% cap rate, so amortizing debt pulls the cash yield below the unlevered yield. Leverage is only positive on an interest-only basis, and only if the rate is under 6.70%.
- **Returns:** a 4% to 6% cash-on-cash return means the return has to come from NOI growth or a lower exit cap, not from day-one cash flow.

**What would change the answer:**
- **Asset type and market.** A 6.70% cap is attractive for a well-located industrial or multifamily asset and unremarkable for office or weaker retail.
- **T-12 quality.** Trailing NOI is not underwritten NOI. I would check for one-time income, below-market expense items, a property tax reassessment at sale, and whether management fees and reserves are included.
- **Lease rollover and in-place rents versus market.** These decide whether the growth story is real.
- **Actual debt terms.** Rate, interest-only period, and proceeds move the table above materially.
