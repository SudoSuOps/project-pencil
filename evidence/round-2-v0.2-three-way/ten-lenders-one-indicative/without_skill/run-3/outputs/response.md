**Short answer: you have one lead, not a loan.** Financing is not lined up. The honest description is "one of ten lenders has shown preliminary interest at 65% LTV, 6.4%, 5-year, subject to everything."

## What an indicative quote is

Indicative terms are a lender's non-binding first look, usually based on the broker's package and before any real underwriting. The lender can change or withdraw them at no cost. The path from here to money looks like this:

1. **Indicative quote** — you are here.
2. **Signed term sheet or application** — the borrower signs and posts a deposit for third-party reports. This is still typically non-binding on the lender.
3. **Underwriting and third-party reports** — appraisal, environmental, property condition, title and survey, plus review of the sponsor's financials, rent roll and leases.
4. **Credit or committee approval.**
5. **Commitment letter** — the first point at which "financing is lined up" is a defensible statement, and even then it carries conditions.
6. **Loan documents and closing.**

Loans most often get cut or die in steps 3 and 4, and you haven't started them.

## What the 1-in-10 tells you

- **You don't yet know what the other nine said.** Hard passes, still reviewing, and no reply are very different situations. Get a lender-by-lender status from the broker, with the reason for each pass.
- **If most of them passed, that is information about the deal.** Common reasons are the price, the in-place cash flow, the asset or market, and the sponsor. The same issue can resurface in the one remaining lender's underwriting.
- **One quote means no leverage and no backup.** You cannot negotiate terms, and if this lender re-trades or drops out you start over, probably with the contract clock running.

## What the quote doesn't tell you

Three numbers are not a loan. These are the open items that decide whether it works:

- **Sizing constraints.** 65% LTV is a ceiling, and it applies to the lender's appraised value, not the contract price. Proceeds will be the lowest of LTV, DSCR and debt yield.
- **Amortization and interest-only.** At 6.4%, the loan constant is about 7.5% on a 30-year schedule and about 8.0% on a 25-year one.
- **Whether DSCR cuts the loan.** At a 1.25x minimum DSCR, the property needs roughly a 6.1% cap rate on lender-underwritten NOI to support the full 65% on 30-year amortization. That rises to about 6.5% on 25-year and falls to about 5.2% if interest-only. If the purchase cap rate is below that, expect fewer proceeds than 65%.
- **Rate.** Find out whether 6.4% is fixed or a spread over an index that floats until rate lock, and when the lock happens.
- **Recourse.** Full, partial or non-recourse with carve-outs, and who signs the guaranty.
- **Costs and structure.** Origination and exit fees, prepayment terms, reserves and escrows, cash management, and any net worth or liquidity covenants.
- **Timing.** Whether this lender can close inside the contract's deadlines.

## The "financing is lined up" statement

This is the part I would deal with first.

- **It overstates the position.** If the seller is relying on it to grant extensions, pass on backup offers, or let a financing contingency lapse, the buyer is creating a credibility problem and possibly a legal one. Counsel should weigh in on any written representation.
- **Check the contract.** Confirm whether there is a financing contingency, when it expires, when the deposit goes hard, and what the closing date is. If the contingency is gone or about to expire, the buyer's deposit is riding on a single uncommitted lender.
- **Say something accurate.** For example: "We have indicative terms from a lender and are moving to a term sheet; we expect a commitment by [date]." That is true and still sounds like progress.
