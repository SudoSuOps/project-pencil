## 1. About You: parties, authority, capacity

### Frame the brief

Settle these five before anything else. If one is missing, state the
assumption used.

| Item | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who reads or hears this? |
| Decision | What do they have to decide, and by when? |
| Stage | Pre-LOI, LOI, PSA negotiation, diligence, post-diligence, closing? |
| Disclosure scope | What may be shared, and with whom? |
| Decider | Who has authority to decide? |
| Engagement | Who commissioned the examination, and which side does it serve? |
| Recipients | Who is authorized to receive findings? |

### Who is at the table

Ask, and record the answer with its tag:

- Who is at the table, and why are they there?
- Who can sign?
- Who takes title (which entity)?
- Who brings the equity?
- What has to happen to close?

### Counterparty discovery questions

Use the ones the situation calls for. Each answer is REPORTED until checked.

- What have you actually closed? Which transactions, entities, and
  principals, and what closing evidence can be supplied?
- What is the strategy: stabilized income, value-add, distressed or
  opportunistic, or something else? A strategy is not misconduct.
- Are you the principal purchaser, a sponsor, a contract assignor, or an
  intermediary? Who ultimately takes title and provides the capital?
- Are you a cash buyer? What shows accessible capital, who owns or controls
  the account, how much, available when, with what limits?
- Are you using a lender? Which one? Is it a direct capital source or a
  broker placing the request with several institutions?
- Is this a 1031 exchange? Are you selling the relinquished property or
  buying the replacement? Who is the qualified intermediary, and where does
  the exchange stand?
- What conditions, third-party approvals, contingencies, or decision rights
  could change your ability or willingness to close?

Ask for the least intrusive evidence that answers the question. Third-party
confirmations (a bank, a lender) need the party's authorization.

### Roles to keep distinct

| Role | What it means | Common confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Principal | Party whose money and name are in the deal | A broker or sponsor speaking as if they are the principal |
| Sponsor | Organizes the deal, may bring little of the equity | Sponsor's track record treated as proof of funds |
| Assignor | Holds a contract position intending to assign it | Presented as the buyer; the end buyer is unknown |
| Broker | Intermediary; represents someone or no one | "I have a buyer" with no mandate or named principal |
| Funding source | Whoever actually wires equity or debt | Named loosely ("my investors", "family office") |

One person can hold more than one role. Record each role separately with its
own evidence.

### Capacity for this deal

Assess whether this party can perform on this transaction now:

- Funds: proof of funds authenticated? Amount vs. equity required? Date of
  the proof? Whose account?
- Authority: documented right to sign for the title-taking entity
  (resolution, operating agreement excerpt, authorization letter)?
- Path to close: any approvals, partners, or lender steps still required?

Track record is context. Record it as a fact ("no prior closings reported")
and do not let it lower the capacity finding when funds and authority are
verified. Equally, a long résumé does not substitute for current proof.
If experience matters to a specific counterparty (a lender's sponsor
requirements, for example), note that as a dependency owned by that
counterparty, not as a PencilBot judgment.

### Sparse facts

Deliver a first pass with what exists, then ask at most three questions.
Choose questions whose answers would change the next move, for example
whether a call is exploratory or mandate-backed. Make each a single
question, not a bundle. Skip questions that are merely nice to know, and ask
none when the record already supports the next move.

## 2. Deal color and evidence tagging

### What to capture

Strategy, stated motivation, authority, capital path, timing, dependencies,
unresolved claims.

### Tagging rules

- **VERIFIED**: the evidence relevant to that exact claim was checked in this
  work. Say what was checked.
- **REPORTED**: stated by a person or asserted in a document that has not
  been checked against a source. Attribute it: who, when, how.
- **INFERRED**: a conclusion from other facts. List them. Never tag an
  inference as VERIFIED because its inputs are verified.
- **UNKNOWN**: needed and absent. Name what would resolve it.

Rules that prevent the usual slips:

- A call note verifies the statement, not the assertion. Write both lines
  when it matters: `VERIFIED: caller said X on [date]` and `REPORTED: X`.
- An OM is a marketing document. Its figures are REPORTED until reconciled
  to the rent roll, T-12, or leases.
- A screenshot, forwarded email, or unsigned draft is REPORTED unless its
  authenticity was checked.
- A document being supplied is not the same as it being read. Tag VERIFIED
  only for the parts actually examined.
- Stated motivation is always REPORTED or INFERRED. Motive is not
  observable.

### Citation format

`[Document, version or date, page / section / row]`

Examples: `[PSA, executed 2026-03-02, §4.2]`, `[Rent roll, 2026-01-31, row 14]`,
`[Call note, 2026-02-10, caller statement]`. If a locator is not available,
cite what is and say the locator is missing.

### From color to call

The discovery-to-communication sequence:

1. **Capture the color**: who the parties are, why they may transact,
   capital source and conditions, strategy, timing, execution dependencies,
   unresolved uncertainties.
2. **Verify claims**: tag each, and keep source, date, authority, and
   permitted uses.
3. **Establish the reason for the call**: a specific, truthful, relevant
   purpose the evidence supports. Do not assume the owner's motivation or
   willingness to transact.
4. **Prepare the conversation**: questions appropriate to the relationship
   and the permission in hand. Plan to listen for new objectives and
   constraints, and to keep what the person says separate from what it might
   mean.
5. **Update the record**: turn call notes into attributed discoveries,
   verification tasks, exceptions, and next actions. Statements stay
   REPORTED until checked.
6. **Examine the property when warranted**: tie the asset and its economics
   to the counterparty and execution picture, and re-test whether the
   assignment is defendable.

How strong an opening the record earns:

| The record shows | The call can be |
|---|---|
| Material color supported, exceptions disclosed | Specific: name what is verified and say what is not ("game on") |
| Color mostly REPORTED | Exploratory: say what was heard and from whom, as heard |
| Little or nothing supported | A learning call about the owner's own plans, with no reference to a buyer or opportunity |

The opening never borrows certainty the record does not have. That covers a
buyer, a mandate, an offer, a funding source, approved financing, a market
fact, the owner's motivation, and a prior relationship with the person being
called.

### The say-out-loud line

Sort every material piece of color onto one side:

- **Can say out loud**: supported well enough that the user could defend it
  if challenged, and within the disclosure scope.
- **Verify first** (or "not yet"): everything else. For each item, name what
  verifying it would let us decide.

Rules for the line:

- A REPORTED claim can be said out loud only as what it is: "I've been told
  by a broker that…". It cannot be said as fact.
- A letter or email from an intermediary about someone else's money is the
  intermediary's statement. It does not move funds to VERIFIED.
- Something can be true and still not sayable, because the user is not
  authorized to disclose it. Keep "I don't know" and "I'm not able to share
  that" as separate answers, and never use one to cover for the other.
- Prepare the truthful answer to the obvious pushback before the call: who
  the buyer is, whether the money is real, whether the caller represents
  anyone. If the honest answer is "I don't know yet", the script says that
  and says what the caller is doing to find out.
- Do not describe an unnamed party with invented colour ("a family office",
  "an institutional group") to make the call sound stronger.

### Contradictions

When two sources disagree, keep both, cite both, and state the difference.
Do not pick a winner unless the evidence decides it. Separate each
observation ("T-12 shows X; OM shows Y") from its interpretation ("OM may
include pro forma rent").

## 3. Asset reconciliation and math

### Reconciliation checks

Run the checks the supplied documents allow, and list the ones that could
not be run.

| Check | Sources compared |
|---|---|
| In-place rent | Rent roll vs. leases vs. T-12 collections |
| Occupancy | Rent roll vs. OM claim vs. T-12 vacancy |
| Lease terms | Lease abstracts or leases vs. rent roll (expiry, options, bumps, concessions) |
| Other income | T-12 line items vs. OM |
| Expenses | T-12 vs. OM; look for omitted management fee, reserves, tax reassessment |
| Taxes and insurance | Actual bills vs. underwriting, especially post-sale reassessment |
| Unit or SF count | Rent roll vs. OM vs. survey or public record if supplied |
| One-time items | T-12 months with non-recurring income or expense |

### Three NOIs

- **Actual**: what the T-12 or actual statements show.
- **Normalized**: actual, adjusted for documented one-time or missing items.
  List every adjustment with its source.
- **Pro forma**: forward assumptions. List every assumption.

State which NOI every metric uses. Never mix them in one calculation.

### Showing a calculation

For each decision-relevant number:

```
Metric: Going-in cap rate (actual NOI)
Formula: NOI / Purchase price
Inputs: NOI $1,240,000 [T-12, Jan–Dec 2025, total row]; Price $18,500,000 [LOI, 2026-02-01, §1]
Result: 6.70%
```

Use a calculator or code when available. Round only at the end and state the
rounding.

### Common formulas

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Cap rate | NOI / price |
| Value at cap rate | NOI / cap rate |
| DSCR | NOI / annual debt service |
| Debt yield | NOI / loan amount |
| LTV | Loan amount / value or price (say which) |
| Cash-on-cash | Pre-tax cash flow after debt service / equity invested |
| Breakeven occupancy | (Operating expenses + debt service) / potential gross income |
| Price per SF or unit | Price / SF or units |
| Expense ratio | Operating expenses / effective gross income |

Units matter: annual vs. monthly, per-SF vs. total, percent vs. decimal.
State them.

### What moves the answer

Say which input the result is most sensitive to, using inputs that are in
the record. If the record gives a range (two bids, two rent figures), show
the result at each end.

### Missing inputs

Leave the metric open and say what the input would decide: `DSCR: OPEN —
needs annual debt service (loan amount, rate, amortization). With it we can
say whether the deal clears a lender's coverage test.`

Do not fill the gap with a market assumption and then conclude from it. That
covers debt terms, return hurdles, cap rates, market rents, funding dates,
repair costs, and prices. A verdict built on an invented input is not
defendable, however reasonable the input looks. For the same reason, do not
propose a price target, a counter, or a retrade number unless inputs in the
record support the math behind it. When someone else proposes a number,
show what it would take for the record to support it.

### Sensitivity scenarios

A scenario is allowed when it helps the user see what an open input would
do. It is a different thing from the deal's record and must stay visibly
separate.

- Head it `SCENARIO — not in the deal record`.
- List each assumed input and mark it as an assumption.
- Show the result, ideally across a small range, so no single assumed value
  reads as the answer.
- Keep scenario results out of the finding, the status, the deal record, the
  work record's "what changed", and any script or external draft.
- Do not convert a scenario into a recommendation ("so offer $X").

If the user supplies an assumption and asks for the math, run it, label it
as the user's assumption, and keep it out of the record until it is
supported.

## 4. Capital ladder and contractual clock

### Capital ladder

Place each source at the highest stage the evidence supports, and no higher.

| Stage | What it supports | Evidence | What it does NOT establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broker indication | A proposed financing route | Broker email or call note | An approved or committed lender |
| Indicative lender terms | Proposed economics and preliminary conditions | Quote or term sheet from the lender, usually marked non-binding | Loan funding |
| Preliminary credit approval | Progress through a specific approval process | Lender communication stating it | That all conditions are satisfied |
| Conditional commitment | A stronger documented financing path | Signed commitment letter and its conditions list | Unconditional disbursement |
| Cleared conditions | Higher execution readiness | Lender confirmation per condition | A completed closing |
| Funded | Money wired | Wire or closing statement | |

When describing a stage in a brief, say what it does not establish as well
as what it supports.

### The lender is a second About You

A lender named by the buyer gets its own examination. A mortgage broker
shopping several banks, a balance-sheet bank, a life company, a debt fund,
and private capital can each be a credible path, but each has different
underwriting, approval, and funding dependencies. Identify the actual
funding decision-maker and the approval path.

Capture what is available, tagging each item: source institution,
intermediary roles, named contact, term-sheet date and expiry, borrower
entity, amount, leverage, recourse, rate structure, appraisal and
environmental or title requirements, credit committee steps, estimated
funding date.

Rules:

- Count of lenders shopped is activity, not progress. Ten lenders approached
  and one indicative quote equals one source at "indicative lender terms".
- Indicative terms are not a commitment. Do not use "approved", "committed",
  or "financed" below the conditional-commitment stage.
- For equity, apply the same discipline: stated interest, soft circle,
  signed subscription or commitment, funds in account.
- Record each source's expected timing and tag it. Lender timelines given
  verbally are REPORTED.

### Clock map

Build from the operative document and every amendment supplied.

| Milestone | Date or Day N | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effective date | | | |
| Diligence expiry | | | |
| Deposit goes hard / additional deposit | | | |
| Notice deadlines | | | |
| Extension options (cost, notice) | | | |
| Financing contingency, if any | | | |
| Estimated funding | | | |
| Closing | | | |

Status values: `EXECUTED` (in a signed document), `PROPOSED` (in a draft or
LOI term not yet signed), `ASSUMED` (PencilBot or user assumption),
`REPORTED` (stated, undocumented).

Procedure:

1. Identify the operative document and list all amendments by date and
   signature status.
2. Record how the document counts days (calendar or business, when Day 1
   starts, what happens on weekends and holidays). If the supplied text does
   not say, mark day-count as an open item rather than assuming.
3. For each date, note whether it is fixed (a calendar date) or defined by
   reference to another date or event (so many days after effective date,
   after diligence expiration, after a notice). When a reference date moves,
   recompute every date that hangs off it, show the arithmetic, and show the
   earlier value beside the new one. Whether a dependent date actually moved
   is a reading of the contract: present the computed result as what the
   text appears to produce, and list it for counsel to confirm. Check
   calendars kept by others (broker, title, lender) against the result and
   name any that are out of step.
4. Lay capital timing against the clock. Compute every gap between when
   money is expected and when the contract requires it, against each date
   still in question.
5. For each gap list what would close it: extension right, earlier funding,
   bridge, renegotiated close, and what each requires.
6. List third-party dependencies with owner and lead time: counsel, title,
   survey, engineering, environmental, lender, decision-makers.

### The hard-deposit cliff

Diligence expiration is often the point where the deposit becomes
nonrefundable, which makes the days just before it a negotiating inflection
point. When a request or exception lands near that date, show the days
remaining, what the supplied contract text says happens at expiration (cite
it), and which decisions have to be made before then. How the deposit
actually operates, including exceptions, notices, and remedies, depends on
the signed PSA and is counsel's to confirm.

### Conflicting documents

When a later amendment conflicts with the original timeline, cite both
provisions, state both dates, and note signature status of each. Present the
later signed amendment's date as the one that appears to control, flagged
for counsel confirmation. Which provision is operative, and what rights or
remedies follow, is counsel's call.

### 1031 milestones

If a party reports a 1031 exchange, establish which leg this deal is for
that party (selling the relinquished property or acquiring the replacement),
who the qualified intermediary is, and where the exchange stands. Track its
identification and exchange deadlines as a separate schedule, tagged
REPORTED unless exchange documents were checked. Exchange timing is set by
tax rules, not by the PSA, and the dates must be verified for the party's
actual facts. Show where they intersect with the PSA clock, but do not merge
them. Refer exchange mechanics to the party's qualified intermediary and tax
advisor.

## 5. Exception register and price-reduction analysis

### Exception register

One row per material exception:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Exception | What is wrong or unproven |
| Evidence | Citation and tag |
| Impact | On price, proceeds, timing, or ability to close |
| Owner of next step | Named person or role |
| Dependency | What it waits on |
| Decision deadline | Date or Day N, with source |
| Possible cure | What might resolve it |
| Cost / time to cure | Only where the record supports a figure; otherwise "not yet supported" |
| Severity and why | How serious, with the reasoning, not just a label |
| Affected workstreams | Which other specialists or approvals it touches |
| Disclosure | Who should receive it, who has, and the state (below) |
| Decision authority | Who decides what to do about it |
| Disposition | How it ended, once it has |

Treat this as a live ledger, not a static checklist. Update rows as facts
change and keep the history.

### Disclosure states

`SENT` → `ACKNOWLEDGED` → `REVIEWED` → `ACCEPTED` → `CURED` → `CLOSED`

Each is a separate event that needs its own evidence, date, and actor.
Record only the state the record supports. An email going out is SENT. It is
not acknowledgment, approval, waiver, or acceptance of risk.

### Latency

Where the record allows, note two gaps for a material finding:

- **Discovery latency**: when the fact became knowable vs. when it was
  found.
- **Decision latency**: when it was known vs. when it reached someone with
  authority to decide.

Long latency is where preventable pain comes from. Report the dates; do not
assign blame.

### Cast of characters

Every participant has a scope, a deliverable, authority, a lead time, and
dependencies. One finding often moves several workstreams.

| Participant | Typical responsibility | Depends on / escalates |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer and seller | Commercial approvals, deposits, elections | All material exceptions and negotiated changes |
| PSA counsel | Contract, notices, rights, deadlines | Timing and rights issues |
| Title / survey | Commitment, exceptions, survey, endorsements | Legal access and lender requirements |
| Inspection / engineering | Physical assessment, repair estimates | Cost to cure and closing economics |
| Environmental | Site assessment, remediation issues | Lender, insurance, and legal implications |
| Lender / capital team | Underwriting, credit, funding conditions | Asset reports, borrower equity, schedule |
| Examiner | Surfaces evidence, dependencies, disclosures, options | Does not own the parties' decisions |

Examples of cross-effects to check: an environmental finding can alter
lender approval; a title exception can require survey and counsel work; a
physical deficiency can alter pricing, reserves, or financing.

"Material" means it could change price, timing, or whether the deal closes.
Raise exceptions as soon as they appear; an early exception has more cures
available than a late one.

### Price-reduction (haircut) analysis

```
Ask:                      $A   [source]
Documented costs:         $D   [itemized, each with source]
Modeled exposure:         $M   [each with assumption; omit if none]
Remaining to explain:     $A − $D − $M
```

Rules:

- Documented means a bid, report, invoice, or estimate was supplied. A
  verbal figure is REPORTED and goes on its own line.
- Show the unexplained amount as a number and as a share of the ask.
- Do not characterize motive (retrade, bad faith, leverage). State that the
  reason for the unexplained amount is not in the record and list what the
  buyer could supply to support it.
- The decision to grant, counter, or refuse belongs to the authorized human.
- The unexplained balance is not automatically unjustified or justified. A
  late request can reflect newly found defects, financing uncertainty,
  changed economics, bargaining leverage, or a mix. Decompose it into
  verified costs, modeled exposure, conditions, and negotiation assumptions.
- Record the claimed basis, the timing of the request against the clock, the
  source evidence, lender effects, and the parties' responses.
- A value-add strategy is different from a pattern of late retrading, and
  neither is by itself evidence of bad faith.

## 6. Validation

Before issuing a brief:

1. **Recompute** each key number from its source inputs. Record pass or
   fail.
2. **Citation check**: for each citation, confirm it supports the exact
   claim, not just the topic.
3. **Conflict check**: list every date and figure that appears with more
   than one value across sources.
4. **Tag audit**: confirm nothing tagged VERIFIED rests on a statement or an
   unread document.
5. **Language audit**: scan for words that overstate stage or certainty
   ("approved", "committed", "confirmed", "cash buyer", "motivated seller")
   and confirm each is supported.
6. **Scope audit**: for external drafts, confirm nothing exceeds the stated
   disclosure scope.

7. **Invented-input audit**: confirm every input behind the finding is in
   the record. Anything assumed belongs in a labeled scenario or comes out.
8. **Settled-language audit**: confirm no contract dependency awaiting
   counsel is written as settled.

In a short answer, report only what failed or remains unchecked. In a full
brief, report three lists: passed, failed, unchecked. Label the review
`Self-check (same session)`. Use "independently validated" only when a
separate reviewer or separate review pass actually occurred, and name it.

Do not give a numerical probability of closing. A defensible probability
would need labeled historical outcomes and calibration that this skill does
not have.

A real validation challenges the work. It recalculates, tests citations,
looks for contradictions, and surfaces disagreement. A second pass that
merely agrees is not validation.

### Assigning status (full brief only)

Two separate fields. Give one line of reasoning for each. Short answers do
not carry status labels; they name the open facts directly.

| Field | Status | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Examination | `DEFENDABLE — EXAMINATION` | Evidence, reasoning, and disclosure record support the findings made |
| Examination | `OPEN — [missing fact] → [what it would decide]` | A material claim cannot be substantiated yet. Always name the fact; never the bare label "insufficient evidence" |
| Execution readiness | `DEFENDABLE WITH CONDITIONS` | Material exceptions are disclosed and bounded; named decisions or cures remain open |
| Execution readiness | `NOT YET DEFENDABLE — EXECUTION` | A material unresolved issue prevents support for readiness |

If execution was outside the request, write "Execution readiness: not
examined". If no material exceptions are open, say that in plain words
rather than inventing a label. A status describes the record on the date of
the brief and should be reassessed when facts change.

## 7. Pencil corrections

Triggered by "Pencil correction".

1. Apply the correction and re-run any calculation it touches.
2. Lead the response with what the correction changes in the decision or
   the call. If the conclusion was open before and is still open, say so and
   say what the correction did change.
3. Write the correction entry (template in `output-templates.md`). The entry
   carries the original finding and the corrected calculation, old → new,
   once. Do not repeat the figures in a separate table or section.
4. A correction to one input does not license filling in others. If the
   corrected number still cannot produce a verdict without a missing input,
   name that input as the next evidence request.
5. Classify:
   - **Deal-specific**: true for this deal only.
   - **Proposed rule**: the correction would improve future work on other
     deals. Propose it only when the case supports generalizing. Write the
     rule in one or two sentences, add one test in the fixture format from
     `evals.md`, and wait for approval.
6. Until approved, a proposed rule is not doctrine. Apply it to the current
   deal only if the correction itself requires it.

Limits: work from this conversation and any supplied log. Do not claim
memory of corrections from other chats. Do not edit the skill's files
silently. On request, assemble a versioned change proposal (template in
`output-templates.md`) containing approved corrections and regression tests.
